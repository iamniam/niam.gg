title: "what if east brunswick’s next chapter were built on its parking lots?"

growing up in east brunswick, i got to know the destinations along route 18 by what i could do there. midstate had groceries and everyday shops. brunswick square had the movies. around summerhill, there were restaurants, a gym and places to run errands. on a map, those destinations look close. on foot or on a bike, a parking lot, a driveway and a difficult crossing can make even the shortest trip feel like a detour.

after i shared my first sketches, a question from the comments stuck with me. if i’m driving to shoprite in the rain, where am i supposed to park? that turned a broad idea about what we could build on already-paved land into a question about the whole trip, from the turn off the road to the distance you carry the groceries. i went back to the property site plans and drew around the building footprints, boundaries and access roads. for the high school, i worked from the district’s proposed campus layout.

the site concepts below show how that question changes the design from one property to the next. each explores a different piece of a town-wide network. the storefronts, paths, parks and transit stops only add up to a walkable neighborhood when the routes between them work.

## brunswick square could become a neighborhood

the developer’s november 2025 lease outline drawing gives us a baseline for the comparison. it shows how much of the mall property is devoted to surface parking and how far its destinations sit from the surrounding streets. the drawing is a developer exhibit for leasing and redevelopment.

in my concept, the mall and its businesses remain at the center. mixed-use buildings line parts of route 18 and rues lane. townhomes, cottage and bungalow courts, multiplexes and stacked flats create a transition along the back. to the east, a park with a playground and basketball court connects to a market and event plaza. a bus park-and-ride near route 18 puts regional travel within walking distance of the mall, park and new homes.

near olive garden, the road curves back into the internal network so drivers can keep going without cutting through the restaurant’s parking lot. on the other side, a driveway ends at habit burger, giving the park more continuous space. those details let the circulation loop serve stores and deliveries while people gain direct routes through the property. the drawing explores three independent garages, with elevator and stair access. the replacement parking shown is a design target. counts, peak demand, ramp grades, cost and accessible routes would determine what gets built.

## two ways to build around everyday destinations

at midstate, the anchor is the grocery trip. the midstate place concept keeps shoprite and surrounding businesses, puts homes over shops around courtyards, and adds a small public park along west prospect. if the side streets and connected internal drives can handle access, closing direct route 18 driveways would reduce conflicts along the protected cycle lane. the concept puts a proposed bus shelter near the frontage and secure cycle parking near shops and homes, including space for cargo and adaptive cycles.

if you’re driving to shoprite in the rain, the concept has you enter a garage from an internal street and come up by elevator or stairs near the storefronts. the connected drives still let you visit several stores in one trip. another commenter asked whether their tall wheelchair-accessible van could fit. the route needs enough clearance at the entrance and along the drive, space to deploy a ramp at the stall, and a step-free connection to the store. those details need to be checked together during design.

i also want older residents, families with children and people who use power wheelchairs, mobility scooters or adaptive cycles to have a direct, comfortable route. separate sidewalks and cycle paths, smooth crossings and secure parking with room for larger cycles can give more people the option of picking up groceries without arranging a ride. that independence can help keep transportation costs down for people who would otherwise need a vehicle for the trip.

brunswick commons has a different job. with big stores, a gym and several destinations spread across the site, the question is how to give the property a useful internal street network. the concept extends highland street, shows an interchange for local and corridor buses, and plots courtyard apartments, offices over shops and a smaller townhome block. loading, garage entrances and everyday crossings need to work at the same time, particularly on a busy retail weekend.

## the summerhill properties should connect across their boundaries

summerhill plaza shows how much can change at the front of an existing shopping center. three- and four-level courtyard buildings could bring shops and front doors closer to the street. planted public spaces would give people somewhere to sit, and the service aisles that keep the center running would stay useful. the drawing retains one route 18 driveway and the existing jughandle. that driveway is precisely where the proposed sidewalk and protected cycle lane need a visible, continuous crossing.

## the high school makes the network useful beyond shopping

the high school sketch addresses a different morning peak. it explores a compact school near summerhill road, a dedicated bus loop, connected athletics fields and outdoor learning space. student walking and cycling routes should connect to surrounding neighborhoods and continue past driveways, with secure parking near the entrances students actually use. the garage beneath parts of the campus is a possibility to test against cost, grading, field design and daily school operations. the campus drawing is an independent mobility and landscape concept. it keeps a small front lot with separate car access and places cycle parking near the athletic fields as well as the school. the concept would phase construction so students could keep using the existing building while the new school and garage go up. the fields could then move onto the former school site, with elevators and stairs serving both the school and central fields.

## transit has to be operating when people move in

more homes can add car trips. the honest response is to study peak-hour turning movements, school travel, deliveries and parking spillover, and to give residents another way to make ordinary trips from the beginning. i would start with frequent bus service, accessible stops and safe walking and cycling connections. route 18 is the candidate for bus rapid transit, with dedicated lanes and signal priority. summerhill road can do a different job as a calmer parallel route linking schools, shops and neighborhoods. rail could be evaluated as service and demand grow.

a bus lane alone won’t get someone home after a late shift. the schedule has to cover evenings and weekends, and the walk from the stop has to work in both directions. that means safe crossings, continuous separate paths for walking and biking, and secure cycle parking where people are going. the township, road agencies, transit operators and property owners would need to agree on funding, phasing and maintenance before the first new residents move in.

driving still has a place in that network. the gain is that a broken car, an expensive repair or a household member who cannot drive no longer has to derail a week. it is also why access across property lines matters. a protected bike path that ends at the first driveway leaves the most difficult part of the trip unresolved.

places like [culdesac tempe](https://culdesac.com/) show one way to build homes near light rail with car sharing and visitor access. east brunswick could start with the trips people already make and choose the mix of parking and transportation that works at each site.

## housing affordability depends on the whole trip

east brunswick can share more of the region’s housing demand by allowing a wider range of homes on its commercial corridors. apartments above shops, townhouses and smaller courtyard homes serve households at different stages of life. [research on new market-rate apartment buildings](https://www.philadelphiafed.org/community-development/housing-and-neighborhoods/supply-shock-versus-demand-shock-the-local-effects-of-new-housing-in-low-income-areas) found that nearby rents were lower than in comparison locations. results vary by market, and income-restricted homes need a defined place in the plan. communities farther from the urban core can offer a lower-cost option when the household budget doesn’t require an extra car and a long drive for every errand.

the economics also matter to the township. keeping existing businesses open gives new shops a base of regular shoppers to build on, and adding residents gives existing shops more people nearby throughout the day. the public question is how much each phase would bring in and what it would cost to serve over time. school capacity, utility upgrades, street maintenance, garage financing and stormwater belong in that accounting alongside projected tax revenue.

## change the rules and test a block first

i would start with a site that has an existing anchor and a plausible connection to bus service. count actual parking use at different times, trace delivery and emergency routes, map drainage and utilities, and check where people already cross. a first block should demonstrate that a business can stay open through construction and that the new paths and stops work on opening day. shared parking and any garages should follow measured demand and a realistic funding plan.

the township could make that work easier by allowing mixed use and a range of housing types under clear standards for height, setbacks, frontage and access. revisiting blanket parking minimums and setting clear approval timelines would help projects that meet those standards move forward. shared parking should be an option where uses peak at different times. predictable approvals can coexist with meaningful public input on the rules, accessible design, fire safety, clean water and wetlands protections. building on pavement also leaves room to plant trees, improve drainage and create usable public space.

with the exception of the credited developer lease exhibit, the drawings are my independent concepts. surveys, engineering, operating agreements and community input would shape what could actually fit. the best test starts with a trip people already make. if you could change one crossing, one bus connection or one stretch of parking lot on the way to school, work or the grocery store, where would you start?
